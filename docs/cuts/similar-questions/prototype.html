We tend to assume that attention is a fixed resource, drained by every notification and refilled only by rest. New work in cognitive science suggests a different picture: attention behaves less like a fuel tank and more like a muscle that responds to how it is used. Subjects who practised sustained focus on a single demanding task showed measurable gains within weeks, while those who habitually split their attention performed worse even on simple tests.

The researchers are careful not to overstate the case. "Training is not a cure-all," the lead author notes. "But the assumption that our capacity is fixed has quietly shaped how offices, schools and even homes are designed — and that assumption now looks wrong."
