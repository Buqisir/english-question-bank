## Source role

This is the highest-impact correction in the supplied research set. Chen states
that the historical dominance of narrative source texts has produced the
mistaken equation “continuation writing = story continuation”. The paper uses
the 2020 curriculum standard to define broader construct coverage and then
tests narrative, expository, and argumentative continuations with the same
students (pp. 11–14).

## Curriculum-to-task construct map

Table 1 maps six curriculum content elements to continuation task design:
theme, discourse, language knowledge, cultural knowledge, language skills, and
learning strategies (pp. 13–14). The cross-type requirements include:

- understanding theme meaning and values through reading/thinking/continuing;
- recognizing purposes, structures, perspectives, and language features of
  different discourse types;
- using vocabulary, grammar, cohesion, and pragmatic resources in context;
- interpreting cultural meaning and expressing culturally appropriate values;
- coordinating receptive and productive skills to narrate, describe, inform,
  argue, and express emotion according to purpose and audience;
- using macro- and micro-structure to organize information logically.

This means discourse purpose and type precede any type-specific checklist.

## Study design

Three tasks use similar length/difficulty and the same two-starter format:

1. a narrative about returning a wallet and reuniting old friends;
2. an expository text explaining preferences for supermarkets versus farmers'
   markets;
3. an argumentative text evaluating an intergenerational home-sharing program.

More than 400 Grade 11 students in ten classes at two eastern-province high
schools participated. Complete data came from 351 students (1,053 responses);
an equal-interval subsample of 117 students, 351 responses, was used for text-
feature analysis. Thirty-six responses per task were sampled for qualitative
analysis. Features were annotated with 88% initial agreement and reconciled by
discussion (pp. 15–16).

The quantitative indicators include lexical diversity/modification, person,
tense, voice, coordinate/subordinate clauses, and cohesion. They are evidence
for construct coverage, not a proposed scoring formula.

## Main results

### Lexis and modification

Raw lexical diversity and modifier frequency do not significantly distinguish
the three types. Qualitative **function** does: narrative modifiers build
action, psychology, scene, character, and atmosphere; expository and
argumentative modifiers more often express degree, frequency, comparison, or
regularity (p. 17). Counting adjectives/adverbs alone therefore loses the
communicative purpose.

### Person, tense, and voice

Pronoun/person patterns follow the source's perspective and communicative
purpose. Narrative responses use past tense and active voice more; expository
and argumentative responses use present/future and passive voice more. Writers
also shift tense appropriately for examples, advice, projection, dialogue, and
changed time-space (pp. 17–18).

### Clause and cohesion patterns

All three types elicit complex clauses, but their functions differ. Narrative
coordination often sequences events/actions; its subordinate clauses commonly
express time or thought. Expository/argumentative relative, conditional, and
causal clauses explain and reason (pp. 18–19).

Narratives rely more on chronology and implicit links through repetition and
reference. Expository/argumentative texts use more explicit causal, additive,
contrastive, and other logical connections (pp. 19–20). Connector count is not
coherence quality; relation and genre function matter.

## Central conclusion

Student responses show systematic, complementary lexical, grammatical, and
discourse differences that broadly align with each type's communicative
purpose and rhetorical conventions. Multiple discourse types therefore cover
more of the curriculum construct and activate recontextualization rather than
memorized narrative routines (pp. 20–21).

Mixed discourse such as narrative-with-commentary or narrative-to-explain can
increase readability or persuasion without erasing the dominant type. Such a
shift should be licensed by the source and starters; it is not automatically a
type violation (p. 21).

## Limits acknowledged by the paper

- two schools in one region limit generalization;
- the feature annotation emphasizes lexico-grammar and discourse, not thinking
  process;
- later work must connect text features to scoring dimensions and score
  interpretation (p. 21).

## Consequences for the later field design (still not a final decision)

1. No universal `plot`, `characterArc`, `actionDetail`, `psychologyDetail`, or
   `environmentDetail` requirement.
2. Start with discourse purpose, dominant type, possible mixed-type shift,
   perspective, intended audience, and macro-structure.
3. Interpret lexis, tense, voice, clause patterns, and cohesion by communicative
   function and source alignment, not raw count.
4. Narrative, expository, and argumentative continuations need shared core
   evidence plus type-conditioned analysis modules.
5. The two starters may intentionally trigger a discourse shift; type
   consistency cannot be reduced to surface sameness.
