## Source role

This excerpt is about **how tasks are made**, not how a student response is
scored. Its sample combines recent national/official adaptation tasks with
selected mock items. It is useful for understanding why the published source
and starters contain deliberate constraints; it cannot prove that every
visible feature is an official scoring subcriterion.

## Item-construction model

The paper organizes item construction into source selection, text adaptation,
and item design. Source materials should be authentic, linguistically accurate,
age-appropriate, manageable, logically coherent, and capable of supporting
continuation. Historically analyzed items are mostly positive, accessible
narratives, but that is a description of the sample, not a universal construct.

The full original is deliberately cut and adapted. The paper identifies eight
adaptation operations: deletion, replacement, annotation, rewording,
reordering, expansion, merging, and splitting. The operations control length,
readability, logical clarity, conflict, and the clues available for later
continuation.

This creates a critical evidence rule: analysis must treat the **published task
text** as authoritative. It must not reconstruct missing material from the
pre-adaptation original or judge a student against the original ending.

## Cut point and conflict

For the narrative examples, the cut commonly leaves the climax/conflict or its
resolution to the writer. A strong cut point preserves multiple plausible
paths while still making the source's central tension and causal possibilities
legible. Added or retained clues can sharpen a character dilemma and explain
why a resolution remains possible.

This supports extracting unresolved states, causal constraints, and available
affordances from the actual prompt. It does not require one canonical climax or
ending.

## Paragraph-starter semantics

The most reusable finding is the starter relation:

- starters provide necessary hints and limits, basic participants/actions/
  emotion/time/place, and a broad discourse frame;
- starter one extends forward from the source;
- starter two both extends forward and constrains what paragraph one must make
  true;
- changes of time, place, participant, or state create the bridge between
  paragraphs;
- a good starter leaves room for different levels and legitimate solutions
  while preventing contradiction.

The paper observes short starters (generally no more than 15 words in its
sample), but this is an item-writing pattern, not a response-quality field.

## Cautions

- Source outlets and old full-text provenance are item-writer metadata, not
  needed to judge a response unless explicitly preserved by the task record.
- Narrative frameworks help inspect narrative item logic but cannot govern
  expository or argumentative continuation.
- “Positive educational value” cannot become a demand for a forced moral tag.
- The paper's mock-item examples must not be represented as national official
  evidence.
